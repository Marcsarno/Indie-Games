# What makes Brotato and Vampire Survivors fun (the user's research brief, 2026-11-16)

The user supplied this brief ("Use this as a guide") when asking to start with the Mage. Condensed here; the
labels (developer evidence / player evidence / design inference) and the source list are the brief's own.

## Main finding
The draw is building a character whose growing power you feel at once. Movement gives moment-to-moment
involvement; upgrades give authorship; escalating encounters test the result; unlocks and other builds give
reasons to return. No single audience: some want relaxed destruction, some hard dodging, efficient shopping,
discovery or odd combinations.
**Working hypothesis:** players return when they have a satisfying experience to repeat and an interesting
possibility they have not tried yet. Ask "what does the player notice, decide, learn and anticipate?"

## Five conclusions to build around
1. Simple input needs meaningful consequences (auto attacks move the decisions to positioning and builds).
2. Power needs a visible payoff: a combination should change what you can do, how safely you move, or how fast you clear.
3. Randomness should create alternatives, not repeatedly withhold the only viable answer.
4. Different builds should change behaviour; more items do not make more interesting runs.
5. A run needs an ending that fits its appeal (enjoying an overpowered build briefly is not standing around with nothing to decide).

## Vampire Survivors vs Brotato
VS: spectacular overwhelming power; movement + successive upgrade picks; combinations and discoveries; risk of
long stretches with little to do. Brotato: assemble a build to survive arena tests; 20-90 s waves with shopping
between (autochess-inspired); character constraints and purchase priorities; risk of menu work and "mandatory" buys.
Choose the emotional promise before the upgrade economy.

## Key ideas
- **Low input, high involvement.** Decisions left: which gap stays open, collect rewards or protect health, step in
  so a short-range weapon reaches, circle / cross / retreat, what the next upgrade should fix. Test: a few minutes
  with a fixed arsenal and no progression; do players find movement techniques?
- **Power works through thresholds the player can perceive:** kill speed (one hit kills), coverage (a protective
  zone), crowd control (can hold a spot), recovery (good play wins back health), chain reactions (one kill sets off
  others). 20% damage and 20% rate = +44% output; let the player feel responsible for it.
- **Build identity > item count.** Judge a build on movement, target priorities, resource decisions, failure
  pattern. Aim for four recognisable playstyles before dozens of item texts. Example dark-fantasy builds from the
  brief: Bone Orbit (hold the orbit distance), Ash Trail (lead foes through your fire), Blood Reaper (dive in to heal,
  disengage), Grave Caller (fight near summon spots).
- **Randomness:** interesting uncertainty and productive compromise are good; "nothing offered helps and my build
  needs one missing piece" is not. Safeguards to test: broad fallback picks, interactions that work through several
  items, no repeated unusable offers, a limited way to redirect offers, rares exciting but not required.
  Adding an item is a design change: which builds does it support, what does it make harder to reach?
- **Shops** are opportunity costs (survive now vs future, reliable vs reroll, deepen vs stay flexible, buy small vs save).
  Watch weak runs: if an early loss makes later choices irrelevant, add a modest recovery.
- **Threats should change movement.** Roles: slow follower (closes routes), charger (punishes predictable travel;
  visible wind-up), ranged (makes distance matter; shots distinct from friendly effects), durable blocker, support
  enemy (reason to approach a group), temporary hazard (clear edge and end). Review deaths in context.
- **Pacing:** what matters is the time between meaningful developments. 12-minute test sketch: 0-1 learn moving and
  the first attack; 1-3 pick a direction; 3-5 meet a weakness; 5-7 a recognisable combination; 7-9 enjoy the power;
  9-11 a distinct final test; 11-12 resolve. Don't cancel every power gain by scaling enemies to match.
- **Progression:** player knowledge, new possibilities (unlock answers "what can I try now?"), permanent power
  (answers "what frustration does this soften?"). Optional goals should suggest different play, not chores.
- **Maps/objectives** earn their cost only when terrain changes priorities; start with one optional objective that
  competes with safety (hold a ritual circle, escort, grab a reward before its route closes).
- **Feedback answers:** did my attack connect, did it achieve something, what hurt me, where is safe next. Emphasis
  in proportion to importance. Keep the player and hostile shots readable under friendly effects; stress-test the
  strongest build on the target phone; frame drops are not proof of power. Portrait phone: side room to escape,
  finger cover, upgrade text size on a real phone.
- **Complaints to watch:** builds feel the same; the needed offers never come; damage feels sudden or unavoidable;
  the end feels passive; more unlocks dilute the pool.

## Recommended prototype and playtest
Scope: one arena, three distinct character rules, six mechanically different weapons, a compact upgrade pool,
several enemy roles, one final encounter. Order: (1) movement, hit feedback, coverage, understandable damage;
(2) one upgrade that visibly changes combat; (3) three combinations with different movement or resource priorities;
(4) a draft or shop with real alternatives; (5) beginning, pressure, payoff, ending; (6) a small unlock layer.
Record: time to the first upgrade noticed in combat; time choosing vs navigating menus; moments with no useful
option; plan changes after an unexpected offer; when the run first felt won or lost; cause of death and whether the
player can explain it; whether a second run is asked for unprompted; the idea they want to try next.
Post-run questions: When did you feel most powerful? Which choice made the biggest difference? Was there a point
where you were waiting rather than playing? What would you change next time? Would you play again now, and why?
**Next decision the brief recommends:** choose whether the game promises relaxed destruction, build optimisation or
active survival, then prototype one complete run around that promise.

## Checklist
- [ ] Audience and ordinary-run promise explicit
- [ ] Movement and damage understandable without upgrade interruptions
- [ ] Early upgrades have a noticeable effect
- [ ] Several builds change behaviour, not just numbers
- [ ] A missing rare item doesn't routinely block a run
- [ ] Enemy combinations create different spatial problems
- [ ] Time to enjoy a successful build
- [ ] The run ends before it becomes waiting
- [ ] Unlocks suggest new experiments
- [ ] Pool expansion doesn't quietly undermine existing combinations
- [ ] Strong builds stay readable and responsive on the phone
- [ ] Testers can explain a meaningful choice and name something to try next

# Expanded edition (the user's second brief, same day): sections 20-29, condensed

## What changed after the extra rounds
- Build variety depends on the enemies and objectives that test it, not only on upgrades.
- A narrow starting character or weapon creates an expectation that the run offers compatible paths.
- A new mechanic can weaken the main activity by giving an easier way round it (DRG: Survivor dropped a dash
  because mining already gave escape).
- Progression can make an enjoyable challenge feel compulsory; optional resets and bounded rewards are alternatives.
- Balance needs the choices players were offered and their circumstances, not just winning builds.

## Round 1, combat and pacing (S14-S16)
- Judge a new ability by the decisions it changes; record movement with and without it.
- Weapon identity, usefulness and power distribution are separate questions; don't equalise shapes away.
- Protect a readable area around the player (20 Minutes Till Dawn raised enemy/floor contrast and moved friendly
  effects off the character). Test full effects vs softer friendly effects near the hero vs simplified effects.
- Two rhythms: movement (routes open and close) and planning (choose, test, revise). Too-frequent choices leave no
  time to feel their consequences.

## Round 2, choices and replayability (S17-S20)
- A starting identity is a promise: give it several viable routes ("choose an identity, discover an implementation"),
  not one exact recipe.
- Predictable vs adaptive builds can be separate modes later; first one satisfying default.
- Pool dilution: with 3 offers drawn from N and K relevant, P(at least one) = 1 - C(N-K,3)/C(N,3): 20/5 -> 60%,
  40/5 -> 34%, 40/10 -> 59%. Audit each new upgrade: which identities use it, when it enters the pool.
- Enemy design can make a rich upgrade system converge (Nova Drift: all-crowd waves made area clear the only answer).
  Encounter matrix: dense weak crowd, durable priority enemy, mixed fast/slow, recoverable sustained pressure,
  temporary objective.
- Winning-build statistics mislead; log offers, picks, state, stage, outcome.

## Round 3, progression and grind (S21-S23)
- A progression screen tells players what they think they must do; check that against the real requirements.
- Look for reward rules that make players stall the climax (DRG: Survivor rewarded fast elite kills).
- Restarting can be content (VS Adventures: contained campaigns with restricted arsenals).
- Test first-time, returning, experienced and post-update players separately.
- Six experiments in order: same encounter with three identities; one alternative enemy composition; relevance
  protection on offers; core movement with/without an extra ability; timely-completion rewards; a small unlock path.

## Melee (sections 25-28, S24-S31)
- Keep movement responsive while attacking (Death Must Die found attack slowdowns clumsy with autoattack).
- There must be a gap between "my attack reaches" and "the enemy hurts me": R > r_player + r_enemy, and startup time
  must fit inside the time an enemy needs to cross that gap.
- Honest hit regions: the visible sweep hits (close targets too), one hit per swing, range to the nearest part of
  big enemies, telegraph / active / recovery separated.
- Targeting: face a nearby enemy while moving freely; keep the target through a swing.
- Every melee class needs one reliable defensive answer (knockback, stagger, a limited guard, recovery on hit, a step,
  a slow); grace after a hit should be visible.
- Recommended first melee class, the WARDEN: auto cleave while moving, broad frontal arc with a rear weakness, one
  visible guard charge on a timer that absorbs a hit and primes a stronger next slash. Second: a DUELIST (narrow
  reach, deliberate repositioning; the best candidate for an optional action button). Backlog: Berserker (rage from
  weapon contact, capped, decays), Grave Reaper (scythe marks).
- Upgrades should change behaviour visibly; keep reach and width separate. Heal per swing capped; bosses need a
  defined response to stagger and repeatable, reachable openings (Brotato fixed whips missing adjacent enemies and
  weapons that could not reach a boss's foot). Delivered DPS = sheet DPS x opportunity x hit success.
- Decision gates: contact damage before a valid swing lands -> fix spacing first; unpredictable attack direction ->
  simplify targeting; a class needs a rare defence -> move a defence into its baseline; bosses drag -> check access;
  runs abandon the main weapon -> audit upgrade coverage; late builds erase all danger -> limit healing/control.
- Post-run questions for melee: Where did you want to stand? Why did that hit connect or miss? What did the shield
  let you do? Which upgrade changed how you moved? When could you safely attack the boss?
