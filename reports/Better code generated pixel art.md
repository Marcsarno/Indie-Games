# Render, Critique, Reshade: Upgrading Claude's Code-Drawn Sprites

Two changes will improve this project's code-drawn pixel art the most, and neither is "training" in the machine-learning sense. The first is a **render-and-look loop**. Claude views a real upscaled PNG contact sheet, scores it against a short multi-dimension rubric for at most 2–3 passes, with deterministic lint checks running before it and a human signing off after. The second is replacing the edge-based shader with **form shading from analytic normals and depth** emitted by each primitive. By the pixel-art community's own definition, an edge-distance shader produces pillow shading. The evidence for the loop comes from adjacent domains. A Claude Sonnet 4.5 self-critique loop raised frontend-code scores **+6.1% over 3 cycles**, while refining without a critic gave only **+1.5%**. A pixel-sprite judge's error against human grades fell from **0.98 to 0.61** once it saw the rendered PNG. An ASCII dump **missed all 11 defects** that a PNG view revealed. The failures are just as clear. **More prescriptive prompts made sprites worse** (human score 4.16 → 3.69), naive turn-by-turn canvas feedback degraded output, and the large RL and fine-tuning gains (SSIM 79.4 → 95.1) require training an open model, which a Claude workflow cannot do. The engine's architecture is already sound: a palette-indexed buffer with banded light is exactly Diablo 1's design (16 light levels as palette remap tables). Its real weaknesses are the shading model, the outline rule and the lack of a seeing loop. One caveat governs everything below: **no study has tested any of these methods on code-drawn pixel sprites with human-rated outcomes**, so much of the transfer to this project is labeled inference.

## Seeing the PNG and scoring it with a rubric beat every prompt tweak

Claude's weights cannot be retrained, so "training" Claude to draw better comes down to four levers. The first is what goes into context: skills, style cards and reference sprites. The second is how output is represented and constrained. The third is the feedback loop. The fourth is the automatic checks that gate and select output. The measured evidence is strongest for SVG, plot and web-UI code. It is weakest, and mostly self-reported, for pixel sprites specifically.

### What has measured support

Structured visual critique of a full rendered image is the best-supported loop for an untrained frontier model. In Amazon's frontend study, a Claude 4.5 Sonnet critic scored rendered pages on 4 dimensions. That judge agreed with humans **69.5% of the time, versus 48.5% for a single-score judge**. With Sonnet as both generator and critic, scores rose **7.584 → 8.048 (+6.1%) over 3 cycles**, and Claude Haiku 4.5 gained +5.7%. The cycle 2→3 gain was only 0.3–0.9%, and tokens grew from ~2.6k to ~45.8k ([arXiv 2604.05839](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05839v1)). IntroSVG ran the same generate-critique-refine loop zero-shot on frontier models and improved FID for GPT-4o (37.80 → 36.34), Grok-4 (41.39 → 32.85) and GPT-5 (35.87 → 32.68). But GPT-5's CLIP score peaked at iterations 1–2 and then fell, so **the latest version is not always the best** ([IntroSVG](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09312)). For plot code, removing PlotGen's visual-feedback agent cost **10–15%** ([PlotGen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00988)). MatPlotAgent's critic helped even without seeing the ground-truth figure ([MatPlotAgent](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11453)).

The critic must see pixels, not a description of them. In Asset Forge's calibration of a Claude Opus judge against human scores on small sprites, mean absolute deviation fell to **0.61 when the judge received the rendered PNG plus an ASCII grid**. It had hovered at 0.86–0.98 with rubric tweaks alone. "The model needs to *see* the output, not just analyze the drawing commands" ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)). jucelinux's code-only drawing pipeline makes the same point more sharply. An ASCII luminance dump **missed all 11 portrait defects** that the PNG view showed, and a whole bench cost ~29,000 tokens as PNGs versus ~240,000 as ASCII ([dev.to/jucelinux](https://dev.to/jucelinux/i-taught-claude-opus-5-to-draw-with-no-image-model-just-code-then-i-asked-for-a-self-portrait-2894)). Images are both more accurate and roughly 8× cheaper than text renderings.

Constraints work best when the renderer enforces them in code, not when prose asks for them. In IntroSVG, standardizing the representation alone raised render success **from 68.41% to 98.62%** and cut FID from 121.50 to 32.15. The standardization used absolute commands, integer coordinates and a fixed canvas ([IntroSVG](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09312)). jucelinux locked the palette, integer positions and per-direction baked facings, and concluded that a hard constraint "does the drawing" ([dev.to/jucelinux](https://dev.to/jucelinux/i-taught-claude-opus-5-to-draw-with-no-image-model-just-code-then-i-asked-for-a-self-portrait-2894)). The closest thing to a measured "skill" result is junyudev/pixel-art, which picks a route by canvas size. At 32 px or smaller, the agent writes a palette plus a text grid and checks it at 1×. Above 32 px, code builds the figure and its lighting, and the agent hand-places faces and hands. The author reports that the skill ranked **1st of 11 entries** on a blind pairwise benchmark, beat its own previous version **15–5**, and that its grid route beat plain Opus **9–1 at 16×16** ([junyudev/pixel-art](https://github.com/junyudev/pixel-art)). These are the author's own numbers with no independent replication.

### What failed or misleads

Longer aesthetic instructions hurt. Asset Forge's human-graded prompt versions fell from **4.16 (baseline) to 3.97 with "detail emphasis" and 3.69 with "category guidance"**. The author concluded that "adding more constraints in the system prompt consistently makes things worse" ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)). This finding fits with jucelinux's once you separate *mechanical* constraints the renderer enforces, which help, from *aesthetic* rule lists in the prompt, which hurt. That reconciliation is an inference.

Naive iteration is also risky. Render-in-the-Loop found that feeding untrained models their incremental canvases turn by turn "brings no consistent gain and often degrades performance": Icon CLIP fell 0.311 → 0.294 and HPS 0.219 → 0.185 ([alphaXiv 2604.20730](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.20730.md)). In one grounding study, iterating over renders of a model's own predictions cut accuracy from **79.6% to 48.7%**. That figure comes from a secondary summary page and should be verified ([awesomepapers summary](https://awesomepapers.io/multimodal/papers/2606.13156)). Models also favor their own output during self-refinement ([arXiv 2412.16829](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16829)).

The judge is generous in a predictable direction. It overrates complex, multi-part subjects: a pickup truck scored 2.3 from humans and 4.3 from the LLM, and a tank 2.4–3.0 versus 4.8 ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)). Metric-only testing has blind spots too. A sprite whose eyes had vanished **passed all 290 automated tests**, and a rotation-pivot bug recurred 4 times and was caught by the human each time, not by Claude ([dev.to/jucelinux](https://dev.to/jucelinux/i-taught-claude-opus-5-to-draw-with-no-image-model-just-code-then-i-asked-for-a-self-portrait-2894)).

### Fine-tuning and RL work, but only if you can train the model

When the model can be trained, rendering rewards produce the biggest numbers in the literature. RLRF took Qwen2.5-VL-7B from SFT-level MSE 8.60 / SSIM 79.4 to **MSE 1.03 / SSIM 95.1**, using pixel-L2, Canny-edge L2, DreamSim and a code-length penalty as rewards. Claude 3.7 Sonnet scored SSIM 69.33 on the same task, though the paper's tables are internally inconsistent ([arXiv 2505.20793](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20793)). None of this is reachable from a Claude workflow. The one pixel-art fine-tune on record, LoRA on Qwen3-1.7B, **improved format but not quality** ([HF Pixel Art Bench](https://huggingface.co/blog/AINovice2005/pixel-art-bench)). The practical analogue for this project is an inference: treat the growing library of parameterized generator code, ramps and lint checks as the thing that "learns", and use automatic checks as a selection signal for best-of-N candidates.

| Show judge the PNG, not commands or ASCII | Asset Forge; jucelinux | MAD 0.98 → 0.61; ASCII missed 11/11 defects | **Adopt** (evidence) |

## The edge-based shader is pillow shading by construction

The community's canonical list of mistakes has stayed stable for over a decade. Cure's Pixel Joint tutorial lists **bad AA, jaggies, bad dithering, banding, pillow-shading, noise and sel-out** under "things to avoid" ([Cure, Pixel Joint](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299)). Two of the seven bear directly on this engine. Cure defines pillow shading as shading that "ignores the light source and follows flat shapes instead of how three-dimensional forms are lit." An automatic shader that darkens pixels by their distance from the silhouette edge does exactly that. Its shade iso-lines run parallel to the outline whatever the light direction. The same construction also produces **banding**, which Cure defines as neighboring pixel runs ending at the same x or y coordinate and "exposing the grid". Concentric rings of shade one pixel apart are the "hugging" variant of it. A frontal light is acceptable "if the forms are respected", so the remedy is form-aware shading, not a different light angle ([Cure, Pixel Joint](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299)).

Outlines are where expert sources genuinely disagree, and the disagreement resolves in this game's favor. Lospec teaches colored and selective outlines but sets one invariant: "an outline should always be darker than both the object AND the background behind it." It also warns that light outline shades fail in dark scenes, where you should "keep solid black outside" and apply selective color to internal lines only ([Lospec, Outlines part 2](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color)). Cure considers sel-out a form of bad anti-aliasing, except where "the background is consistently dark" ([Cure, Pixel Joint](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299)). Darkest Dungeon's Chris Bourassa credits strong linework with keeping a dark game readable ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com:443/articles/the-gothic-sensibilities-of-darkest-dungeon/1100-6424880/)). So the dark **exterior** outline in a torch-lit dungeon is defensible. The problem is uniformity inside the sprite. Lospec notes that "not every part of your object needs to have an outline", that internal separations need only "enough of the line to suggest the separation", and that internal lines should take the color of the part nearest the viewer ([Lospec, Pixel Art Outlines](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines); [part 2](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color)).

Color rules are concrete enough to encode. Slynyrd builds ramps with about **+20° hue shift per step** ("20 is about as high as I go"), saturation peaking mid-ramp and never at 0 or 100%, and brightness rising with smaller steps near the top. Straight, unshifted ramps are "less interesting and harder to harmonize" ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 1](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/1/10/pixelblog-1-color-palettes)). Lospec's 1× test is directly checkable: "you should be able to see the difference between any two colors that touch" ([Lospec](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color)). For animation, Slynyrd recommends strong keyframes that read on their own, 6-frame cycles that "work well in most cases", ~80 ms per frame for an 8-frame run (160 ms if cut to 4), and putting "energy of the motion over smoothness." He also warns that "a single orphan or misplaced pixel is obvious" in a loop ([Slynyrd Pixelblog 8](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2018/8/19/pixelblog-8-intro-to-animation)). Scene readability matters as much as sprite readability. A Polycount critique of a dungeon tileset complained that "all the lighting is similar in brightness and contrast" and that characters were hard to see against the floor ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/2359408)).

The table converts these sources into rules the pipeline can check automatically. The rule text is sourced. **Every numeric threshold is an inferred starting point**, because no source validates thresholds at 24–64 px.

| # | Rule | Automatic check | Starting threshold | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Shade follows light and form, not edge distance | Correlate shade index S with edge distance D and with n·L | Flag if corr(S,D) > 0.6 and corr(S,n·L) < 0.3 | Cure (rule); inference (check) |
| R2 | No banding: parallel staircases between successive shade boundaries | Per row and column, compare run endpoints of adjacent boundaries | Fail at ≥3 consecutive aligned steps | Cure (rule); inference (check) |
| R3 | No noise: single pixels only when purposeful | Pixels with no same-index 8-neighbor, minus tagged eyes and speculars | ≤1–2% of opaque pixels | Cure (rule); inference (threshold) |
| R4 | Clean lines: equal segments, monotonic curves, no L-corner doubles | Run-length trace of 1-px chains; 2×2 L-shape detector on the outline | 0 doubles; straight edges at slopes 0:1, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 1:0 | [Lospec](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines); [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/content/chapter-2-lines-and-curves); [Aseprite Pixel Perfect](https://planet.kde.org/ken-lo-2024-06-10-week-2-recap-aseprite-s-pixel-perfect) |
| R5 | Touching colors are distinguishable at 1× | ΔE (OKLab) for every 4-adjacent pair of different indices | Minimum ΔE set per palette | Lospec (rule); inference (metric) |
| R6 | Outline darker than both object and background | Luminance(outline) < min(neighbor interior, floor band) | Must hold on every lit band | Lospec |
| R7 | No outline between parts that touch in one sprite; internal lines use the nearer or darker part's ramp | Internal-line pixels must belong to a material ramp, not the outline index | 0 pure-outline pixels inside the silhouette | Lospec |
| R8 | Hue-shifted ramps | Per ramp: hue monotonic within ≤20°/step, saturation arched, value monotonic | Adjacent-step ΔL ≥ ~8–10 | Slynyrd (rule); inference (ΔL) |
| R9 | No mixels: one pixel grid | All parts drawn at native resolution; integer nearest-neighbor scaling only at display | Detect aligned 2×2 block regions | [Flowlab](https://community.flowlab.io/t/mini-rant-stop-using-mixels/38287), [Squeakypig](https://squeakypig.itch.io/unicopia/devlog/787703/a-tale-about-chasing-a-pixel-perfect-tail) (rule) |
| R10 | No dithering on characters; on tiles only at transitions | 2×2 checkerboard detector | 0 on sprites; ≤10% of tile area | Cure (rule); inference (threshold) |
| R11 | Sprite separates from floor in value | Mean \|ΔL\| between sprite pixels and the floor tile under it, at each light band | Above a set floor | Polycount critique; inference (metric) |
| R12 | Animation reads from keyframes; no flicker pixels | Per-part pixel volume across frames; isolated pixels that toggle every frame | Volume within ±10–15%; 0 orphan flickers | Slynyrd (rule); inference (threshold) |
| R13 | Silhouette reads as one color | Flat-fill render reviewed by the critic and a human; head and weapon distinct | Human pass | [Gamezxz](https://github.com/Gamezxz/pixel-art-studio) silhouette-first practice; inference |

## Analytic normals and depth are the cheapest path to real form

Asset Forge found that 2D primitives hit a ceiling on complex subjects. Tiles, potions and crowns scored 4.8–5.0 with human graders, but dragons, knights and vehicles became "recognizable blobs" at 32–64 px. A dwarf blacksmith scored 3.0 and a barrel 2.7 ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)). This engine also draws characters from 2D primitives, so the same ceiling applies. The techniques below are ranked by how much they would lift it, per unit of engineering cost. The ordering is my judgment from the sources, not a measured comparison.

**Analytic normals and depth per primitive rank first.** Dead Cells shipped its ~50 px characters by exporting "a normal map" with every frame and shading them with "a basic toon shader" ([Game Developer, Vasseur](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-)). Tools such as Laigter approximate normals from finished art ([Laigter](https://laigter.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Introduction/intro.html)). A code-drawn sprite can do better because each shape's geometry is known exactly. An ellipse can write a sphere-impostor normal: with u = x/a and v = y/b, z = √(1−u²−v²). A limb or capsule can write a cylinder normal from its distance to the segment axis. A plate or blade can write a constant tilted normal with a beveled rim. Each primitive also writes depth. Shading then becomes Lambert n·L, quantized into 3–4 steps of that material's ramp, so the output stays on-palette and passes R1 by construction. Cylinders produce vertical bands that read as form instead of concentric rings. The transfer of Dead Cells' approach to analytic 2D primitives is an inference. One caution: unquantized n·L creates single-pixel bands, so apply a minimum-run-length or majority cleanup to the band index.

**Outlines from depth and normal discontinuities rank second**, because they depend on the depth buffer above. The most-copied 3D pixel-art post shader samples 4 neighbors and draws an outline on the *near* side of any depth jump. It colors the outline `mix(orig, black, 0.4)`, a darkened shade of the underlying color rather than pure black, and adds a one-sided highlight on normal creases ([godotshaders, leopeltola](https://godotshaders.com/shader/3d-pixel-art-outline-highlight-post-processing-shader/)). Pixelat3D, whose user study (n = 62) found a significant preference for its output, outlines with "a darker shade of the closest color" and notes that 8-neighbor detection catches diagonal edges that 4-neighbor detection misses ([SBGames 2026 paper](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbgames/article/download/45414/45175/)). For this engine, that means two rules. The silhouette against the floor stays dark (R6). An arm crossing the torso gets an internal line from the arm material's dark ramp step only where depth actually jumps (R7).

**Palette-ramp light tables rank third**, and the engine is most of the way there. Diablo 1 treats its 256 colors as ramps of 16 or 8 entries. It builds **16 light tables**, each shifting every index `shade × steps / 16` entries down its own ramp, so table 0 is identity and table 15 is all black. It stores light per tile, falls off linearly to `(r+1)×8` sub-tile units, combines overlapping lights by taking the brightest, and interpolates on an 8×8 sub-tile grid as lights move ([DevilutionX light_tables.cpp](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/engine/light_tables.cpp); [lighting.cpp](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/lighting.cpp)). Sprites are lit by an index remap at blit time with no RGB math, and darkened pixels keep their hue identity until they reach black ([scrollrt.cpp](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/engine/render/scrollrt.cpp)). Diablo exempts emissive lava indices from darkening, rotates hell-level light tables so animated colors stay animated in shadow, and creates monster variants as free palette swaps: Ghoul, Rotting Carcass and Black Death are all the Zombie sheet with a different TRN ([monstdat.tsv](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/assets/txtdata/monsters/monstdat.tsv)).

One design tension follows from this, and it is an inference. With ~51 colors, ramps will be about 5–7 steps long rather than Diablo's 16. Form shading consumes 3–4 of those steps before the light table shifts anything. The palette therefore needs a **shared dark tail**: two or three blue-purple near-blacks that every ramp walks off into, as Endesga 32's darkest #181425 does ([Lospec, Endesga 32](https://lospec.com/palette-list/endesga-32)). With that tail, a highlight pixel in the darkest band lands on a harmonized dark instead of clipping to a muddy wrong hue.

**Rig-level posing ranks fourth.** Characters should be posed through a 3D skeleton with two-bone IK and redrawn at native resolution for each direction, never rotated as bitmaps. FABRIK and analytic two-bone IK are standard and cheap. Gait systems lock planted feet to stop sliding and swing feet along arcs ([FABRIK](https://www.andreasaristidou.com/FABRIK); [PIKA](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/sueda/courses/CSCE450/2025S/projects/David_Herring/index.html)). Rotating finished pixels requires RotSprite-style upscale-rotate-downsample, cleanEdge (MIT, runs in the browser) or MMPX (open-source JS, palette-preserving) ([cleanEdge](https://torcado.com/cleanEdge/); [MMPX](https://about.roblox.com/publications/mmpx-style-preserving-pixel-art-magnification)). For code-drawn parts, re-rasterizing the rotated vector definition avoids resampling entirely. Reserve cleanEdge or MMPX for bitmap parts such as weapon icons. Solving IK in iso world space and then projecting keeps feet on the ground plane (inference).

**Layered paper-doll characters rank fifth.** They resolve their draw order through depth. Diablo 2 composites up to **16 layers** (head, torso, legs, arms, hands, shield, 8 specials), using a COF `Priority[direction][frame]` table so a shield can draw behind the torso when facing away ([OpenDiablo2 cof.go](https://github.com/OpenDiablo2/OpenDiablo2/blob/master/d2common/d2fileformats/d2cof/cof.go); [composite_type.go](https://github.com/OpenDiablo2/OpenDiablo2/blob/master/d2common/d2enum/composite_type.go)). LPC's generator does the same with JSON items that carry front and back layers ([OpenGameArt](https://lpc.opengameart.org/comment/92785)). A per-pixel depth buffer from the first technique makes the hand-authored priority table unnecessary.

**A full low-resolution 3D pipeline is the fallback if 2D analytic shading plateaus.** It would use three.js with an orthographic camera, an `antialias:false` render target, MRT outputs for material, normal and depth, and a toon post pass. This is the Dead Cells / t3ssel8r recipe. Its known weakness is flicker: Dead Cells' "flickering pixels were never fully solved" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-)). Grid snapping fixes crawl only under orthographic projection, which is exactly what an isometric camera uses ([Texel Splatting, arXiv 2603.14587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14587)). Voxels are a proven iso route (IsoVoxel, PixVoxel, MagicaVoxel export), but .vox models "can't be animated as 3D models" without splitting them into rigid parts ([IsoVoxel](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Ftommyettinger%2FIsoVoxel); [itch.io devlog](https://fluffy-lotus.itch.io/secret-of-the-basilisk/devlog/45370/converting-vox-files-to-isometric-images)). SDF characters have no cited shipped example. If supersampling is ever used, reduce material IDs by mode or priority per block, not by averaging. That is the cheap analogue of Kopf's content-adaptive downscaling and Pixelat3D's highest-priority-texel rule ([Kopf 2013](https://www.johanneskopf.de/publications/downscaling); [Pixelat3D](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbgames/article/download/45414/45175/)). Bollinger-style mask generators, about 200 lines of JS, are best kept as sub-generators for helmets, shields and monster heads ([zfedoran](https://github.com/zfedoran/pixel-sprite-generator)).

| Rank | Technique | Fixes | Evidence level | Cost here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Analytic normal + depth per primitive, quantized Lambert into ramps | Pillow shading, banding, flat form | Shipped analogue (Dead Cells); transfer inferred | Low: extends existing buffer |
| 2 | Depth/normal-discontinuity outlines, material-dark internal lines | Uniform black interior lines, "sticker" look | Shipped shaders + user study (Pixelat3D) | Low once depth exists |
| 3 | Ramp-structured palette + light-table LUT + emissive exemption | Muddy darkening, unreadable hazards | Primary code (DevilutionX) | Low: mostly palette redesign |
| 4 | 3D rig + two-bone IK, re-rasterize per direction | Rotation artifacts, foot sliding, 8-direction cost | Standard techniques; application inferred | Medium |
| 5 | Layered paper-doll with depth-resolved order | Equipment variety, occlusion | Primary code (OpenDiablo2), LPC | Medium |
| 6 | Ortho WebGL low-res 3D pipeline | Ceiling of 2D primitives | Shipped (Dead Cells); flicker unsolved | High |
| 7 | Voxels / SDF | Organic forms, free iso shading | Tools exist; no rigged shipped case | Medium–High |

## Diablo's numbers translate to 32–40 px heroes, 8 directions and 5–8 light bands

Every reference pipeline here pre-rendered from 3D for the same reason: throughput across many directions and actions. Diablo 1's characters were 3D models "pared down" to 2D ([Destructoid excerpt](https://www.destructoid.com/the-origin-of-diablo/)). Diablo 2 exported 3D Studio Max models at 8 angles for creatures and up to 16 for player characters ([LevelUp](https://www.levelup.com/articulos/la-historia-detras-de-diablo-ii-uno-de-los-juegos-mas-influyentes-de-la-historia/)). Hades I animated in Maya and shipped an "entirely 2D workflow". Hades II moved to real-time 3D to support "twice as many characters" with less memory and proper turns instead of "tank turn" ([DigiPen](https://www.digipen.edu/showcase/news/hades-2-graphic-programmer); [MCV/DEVELOP](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/behind-the-art-of-hades-we-value-artistic-integrity-and-excellence-in-artistic-craft-at-supergiant-however-were-first-and-foremost-a-game-design-lead-team/)). A code-drawn renderer already has the property those studios bought with 3D: directions and frames cost compute, not artist hours. The binding costs here are review effort per frame and pixel stability, so frame counts should be set for readability rather than to save labor.

Diablo 1's data tables give the best frame benchmark. The Warrior has **idle 10, walk 8, attack 16 (axe 20), hit 6, death 20 and cast 20** frames, and the hit lands at a defined "action frame": sword 9 of 16, axe 10 of 20, bow 11, cast 14 of 20 ([DevilutionX warrior animations.tsv](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/assets/txtdata/classes/warrior/animations.tsv)). That puts impact **50–70% of the way through the attack**. Monsters vary (Skeleton 9/8/16/5/16; Zombie 11/24/12/6/16), with a per-animation rate column, so a Zombie's idle plays at quarter speed. Frame widths are 96, 128 or 160 px ([monstdat.tsv](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/assets/txtdata/monsters/monstdat.tsv)). Directions number 8, with turning as `(dir ± 1) % 8`, and missiles use a separate 16-direction set ([direction.hpp](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/engine/direction.hpp); [misdat.h](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/tables/misdat.h)). Floor tiles are 64×32 diamonds, and walls are stacks of 32×32 pieces with left and right transparency flags for cutaway ([dun_tile.hpp](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/levels/dun_tile.hpp)). Diablo 2 widened lighting to **32 light-level remaps** and precomputed its transparency, additive and multiplicative blends as palette LUTs in PL2 files ([OpenDiablo2 pl2.go](https://github.com/OpenDiablo2/OpenDiablo2/blob/master/d2common/d2fileformats/d2pl2/pl2.go)). Dead Cells' characters are about **50 px tall** in game ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-)). Isometric pixel art uses 2:1 lines (~26.6°) rather than true 30°, with 32×16 and 64×32 as the common tile grids ([Construct.net](https://www.construct.net/en/tutorials/creating-isometric-pixel-art-355/page-6)). Drawing 5 directions and mirroring them to 8 is an established asset convention ([OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/content/58-directional-sprite-sets)), but Diablo 2's per-direction layer table shows Blizzard did not mirror.

| Spec | Reference data | Recommendation for 270×480 (inference unless noted) |
|---|---|---|
| Directions | D1: 8 (missiles 16); D2: 8 monsters, up to 16 players | **8** for characters; 16 for projectiles; mirror the *rig*, not pixels, to keep weapon hand correct |
| Character height | Dead Cells ~50 px; D1 frame widths 96–160 px at 640 wide (≈40–67 px when scaled to 270) | **32–40 px** tall heroes and regular monsters (≈7–8% of screen height); 48–64 px for bosses |
| Proportions | 32 px → 3–4 heads; 48 px+ → 4–6 heads ([Pixnote](https://pixnote.net/en/learn/character)) | ~3–3.5 heads at 32–40 px; enlarge hands and weapon |
| Floor tile | D1 64×32; common 32×16 and 64×32 | **32×16** (8.4 tiles across 270 px); 48×24 if more zoom is wanted |
| Idle / walk / run | D1 idle 10, walk 8; Slynyrd 6-frame cycles, 80 ms/frame at 8 | Idle 4–6 at 150–250 ms; walk 6–8 at 100–130 ms |
| Attack | D1 16–20 frames, hit at 50–70% | 6–10 frames, hit frame at ~55–65%, long anticipation and recovery |
| Hit / death | D1 hit 5–11, death 13–24 | Hit 3–4; death 6–10 |
| Light levels | D1 16 tables, linear falloff; D2 32 | **5–8 bands**, per tile with sub-tile interpolation, brightest-wins combine |
| Variants | D1 TRN palette swaps; D2 16-layer composites | Palette-row swaps for monster tiers; layered equipment with depth-resolved order |
| Emissives | D1 lava exempt from light tables, palette cycling | Reserve indices for fire, magic and lava that ignore darkness |

The frame-count rows halve Diablo's numbers. That scaling is a judgment drawn from Slynyrd's advice that "energy over smoothness" matters more at small sizes. It is not a published spec.

## Prioritized action plan for this project

The order follows dependency and payoff. The measurement loop comes first, because without it no later change can be shown to help. The shading and outline fixes follow, then palette and lighting, then rig and animation. The Basis column separates what the evidence directly supports from what I infer by transferring results from adjacent domains or shipped games.

| Priority | Action | Basis | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0-1 | Build a **contact-sheet renderer**. It outputs one PNG per character: 8 directions × all frames, at 1× and 4× nearest-neighbor, each placed on the real floor tile at the brightest, middle and darkest light bands, plus a flat-fill silhouette row. Claude views this PNG, never ASCII. | **Evidence**: judge MAD 0.98 → 0.61 with PNG; ASCII missed 11/11 defects; PNG ~8× cheaper ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/); [jucelinux](https://dev.to/jucelinux/i-taught-claude-opus-5-to-draw-with-no-image-model-just-code-then-i-asked-for-a-self-portrait-2894)) | One command regenerates every sheet |
| P0-2 | Add **deterministic lint gates** that run before any visual review. Hard gates: palette subset, canvas and anchor, no stray alpha. Lint checks: R3, R4, R5, R6, R10, R12 from the rules table. | **Evidence** that tests catch some defects and miss others ([jucelinux](https://dev.to/jucelinux/i-taught-claude-opus-5-to-draw-with-no-image-model-just-code-then-i-asked-for-a-self-portrait-2894)); thresholds are **inference** | Lint runs in CI and blocks sprites that fail a gate |
| P0-3 | Write a **4–5 dimension anchored rubric** of mostly yes/no structural questions: light consistency, silhouette, outline invariant, value separation, animation read. Each critique must name specific pixels or regions. Cap the loop at **2–3 passes**, keep the highest-scoring version, and leave likeness ("does it look like a skeleton warrior?") to a human. | **Evidence**: multi-dimension judge 69.5% vs 48.5% agreement; +6.1% self-critique, plateau after cycle 2; judges overrate complex subjects ([arXiv 2604.05839](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05839v1); [IntroSVG](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09312); [Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)) | The rubric file exists and the loop records scores per pass |
| P0-4 | Package the workflow as a **project skill** with a short prompt. Mechanical rules live in code and data (palette table, ramps, lint); the style card and 2–3 approved reference sprites live in context. Do not grow a long list of aesthetic rules. | **Evidence**: prescriptive prompts 4.16 → 3.69; skill routes 15–5 and 9–1 (self-reported) ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/); [junyudev](https://github.com/junyudev/pixel-art)) | The skill's prompt stays under ~1 page; rules are enforced by code |
| P0-5 | Build a **blind A/B evaluation** with 10–20 fixed sprite briefs. A human judges old pipeline versus new in pairwise comparisons before any pipeline change is accepted. | **Inference** modeled on Pixel Bench's pairwise design ([junyudev](https://github.com/junyudev/pixel-art)) | Every P1 change carries a win/loss record |
| P1-1 | Each primitive writes a **normal and depth** into the material buffer: sphere-impostor ellipses, cylinder limbs, beveled plates. Replace edge-distance shading with quantized n·L from one fixed light, with a minimum-run cleanup. | **Inference** from the pillow-shading definition and Dead Cells' normal maps ([Cure](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=11299); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-)) | R1 and R2 pass; wins the A/B |
| P1-2 | Replace uniform outlines. The exterior stays dark (black or the shared darkest); interior lines appear only at depth jumps, in the nearer part's dark ramp step; run doubles cleanup on all 1-px lines. | **Evidence** for the rules ([Lospec](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color); [Pixelat3D](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbgames/article/download/45414/45175/)); the dark-scene compromise is **inference** | R4, R6 and R7 pass |
| P1-3 | Re-organize the ~51 colors as **hue-shifted ramps** of 5–7 steps per material with a shared 2–3-color dark tail. Make the torch light table an index shift down each ramp, with emissive indices exempt. | **Evidence** for the mechanism ([DevilutionX](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/Source/engine/light_tables.cpp); [Slynyrd](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/1/10/pixelblog-1-color-palettes)); ramp lengths and tail are **inference** | R8 passes; R11 passes at every band |
| P1-4 | Define characters as a **3D rig with two-bone IK**. Rasterize each of 8 directions from the posed rig at native resolution; never rotate bitmaps. Snap the root to integer pixels and keep thin parts at least 1–2 px wide. | **Inference** from D2 and Dead Cells pipelines and the ortho-snapping result ([Texel Splatting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14587)) | The pivot-bug class is gone; R12 flicker check passes |
| P2-1 | Adopt the **frame table** above with explicit hit frames and per-animation rate multipliers. | **Evidence** for D1 data ([animations.tsv](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX/blob/master/assets/txtdata/classes/warrior/animations.tsv)); scaled counts are **inference** | Each action has a declared hit frame |
| P2-2 | Add **layered equipment** with depth-resolved order and palette-row swaps for monster tiers. | **Evidence** for the D2 and LPC architecture ([cof.go](https://github.com/OpenDiablo2/OpenDiablo2/blob/master/d2common/d2fileformats/d2cof/cof.go)) | Swapping armor changes no hand-written draw order |
| P2-3 | Use the **text-grid route** for icons, potions and pickups of 32 px or smaller; keep the construct route plus hand-placed faces and hands for characters. | **Evidence** (self-reported) ([junyudev](https://github.com/junyudev/pixel-art)) | Small items pass lint at 1× |
| P3 | Move to an **ortho WebGL low-res 3D pipeline** only if P1 plateaus in the A/B. | **Inference**; flicker remains unsolved ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/art-design-deep-dive-using-a-3d-pipeline-for-2d-animation-in-i-dead-cells-i-)) | A/B shows a gain over P1 |

The evidence also says what to stop doing. Do not answer bad sprites by appending more aesthetic rules to the prompt. Do not iterate past three passes or always keep the newest version. Do not review sprites as ASCII or rely on tests alone. Do not emit raw per-pixel arrays for sprites larger than 32 px ([Asset Forge](https://matthewdeaves.github.io/assetforge/)). Do not dither moving characters or rotate finished bitmaps. Do not spend effort on fine-tuning or RL, which a Claude-based workflow cannot apply.

## Conclusion

The research shows that the engine's core architecture is already Diablo 1's: palette indices, ramp-walking light tables and banded radius falloff. That design has shipped successfully. Its art suffers from two local choices, edge-distance shading and uniform outlines, which the craft literature names as mistakes, and from the absence of a visual loop that would have caught them. That combination makes the problem tractable. The fix is a shading model that knows each primitive's shape, plus a cheap, measured review loop, not a new renderer. The shift in perspective is that "training Claude" here means accumulating code: normals, ramps, lint rules and a rubric that persist across every sprite. Better instructions and more iterations are not the lever.

The largest unknown is how far a Claude critic can be trusted at 32 px. No study measures VLM judges against human artists on code-drawn sprites at this size, CLIP and DINO metrics are likely weak there, and every published judge overrates complex subjects. A small blind A/B set, judged by a human, is therefore the most important piece of infrastructure. It turns every recommendation above from inference into evidence for this specific game, and it makes the loop itself auditable rather than self-congratulating.
